About Us

About ARCH REACH

ARCH REACH is a national research center that brings together scientists, clinicians, and community members to improve the health and well-being of people living with HIV. Our focus is on understanding and addressing unhealthy alcohol use among people with HIV, in a way that is grounded in and shaped by the experiences of the people most affected.

Alcohol use can make it harder for people with HIV to stay engaged in care, take medication consistently, and manage the other health conditions that can come with aging. It rarely happens in isolation — it's often connected to mental health, stigma, and the broader social and economic conditions in someone's life. ARCH REACH studies these connections and develops, tests, and shares practical ways to help.

Funded by the National Institutes of Health (NIH), ARCH REACH builds on more than a decade of research through the Alcohol Research Consortium in HIV (ARCH) and draws on data and infrastructure from CNICS, a network of HIV clinics across the United States that follows nearly 50,000 people with HIV over time. The center's work is organized around three research projects — ARCH ERA, T-BAI, and ARCH-STAR — supported by three Cores, and guided throughout by a community partner board made up of people with lived experience.

The Cores

Three Cores provide the infrastructure, scientific rigor, and community connection that make ARCH REACH's research possible.

Administrative Core

University of Washington

Keeps ARCH REACH running smoothly — coordinating communication across Cores and projects, overseeing timelines, budgets, and regulatory compliance, and supporting the training of students and early-career researchers. Also convenes the Steering Committee and Program Advisory Committee.

Methods Core

University of Washington

Provides the scientific and statistical expertise behind ARCH REACH's research — designing rigorous studies, developing advanced statistical approaches for how alcohol use, HIV, and social factors intersect, and helping translate findings into real-world practice.

Dissemination Core

Tulane University

Connects ARCH REACH's research with the people it's meant to help — leading the Transdisciplinary Community Partner Board, training researchers in community-engaged methods, and sharing findings with the public, providers, and policymakers.

Project 1

ARCH ERA — Epidemiological Research Arm

University of Washington

Uses data from nearly 50,000 people with HIV to understand how alcohol use affects HIV care, medication adherence, and age-related health conditions over time.

What we're studying

How alcohol use — and treatment for it — relates to staying in HIV care, medication adherence, and age-related conditions like sleep, bone, and kidney problems.

Who's involved

Analysis of CNICS, a network of HIV clinics across the U.S. following nearly 50,000 people with HIV over time.

Why it matters

Identifies which newer treatments and social factors — like housing and discrimination — matter most, so care can be better targeted.

Project 2

T-BAI — Telephone-Based Brief Alcohol Intervention

University of Washington

Adapts an effective in-person and computer-based counseling program for delivery over the phone, then pilot-tests it with patients at Seattle-area HIV clinics.

What we're studying

Whether an effective in-person/computer counseling program for unhealthy alcohol use works just as well delivered over the phone.

Who's involved

Patients and clinicians at UW/Harborview HIV clinics help redesign the program; a small pilot study then tests it with patients.

Why it matters

Makes counseling accessible to people without reliable internet, a computer, or transportation to an in-person visit.

Project 3

ARCH-STAR — Syndemic Conditions Treatment Arm

UC San Diego & University of Miami

Offers layered, "stepped" support for people with HIV whose unhealthy alcohol use occurs alongside depression or anxiety — starting light, and stepping up to combined therapy when needed.

What we're studying

A "stepped" model of care for people with HIV whose unhealthy alcohol use occurs alongside depression or anxiety.

Who's involved

Patients at HIV clinics in San Diego and Miami, starting with lighter-touch online support before stepping up as needed.

Why it matters

Tests whether combined therapy for alcohol use and mental health can be delivered at scale in everyday clinic settings.

Papers

Publications

Peer-reviewed work from the ARCH REACH team, in plain language.

2026

Healthcare Provider Perceptions on Screening and Treatment for Unhealthy Alcohol Use in HIV Primary Care Settings

Kennedy AJ, et al. AIDS and Behavior

Summary: Interviews with 19 healthcare providers across three HIV clinics found that mixed confidence in screening tools, discomfort with alcohol medications, and competing priorities make it harder to address unhealthy alcohol use in HIV care — while embedding alcohol treatment directly in HIV clinics, having a clinical champion, and offering telemedicine made it easier.

2026

Practice facilitation to implement stepped care for unhealthy alcohol use in HIV clinics: study protocol for a type III hybrid effectiveness-implementation study

Chander G, et al. Addiction Science & Clinical Practice

Summary: This paper lays out the design of the ARCH REACH clinical study: testing whether hands-on support for clinic staff, called practice facilitation, helps HIV clinics successfully adopt a "stepped" model of alcohol care that starts with brief counseling and steps up to more intensive treatment for patients who need it.

2025

Brief report: Are changes in cannabis use frequency associated with changes in alcohol use and smoking among people with HIV (PWH) — a substitution question

Whitney BM, et al. Drug and Alcohol Dependence

Summary: Some have hypothesized that using more cannabis might lead people to cut back on alcohol or cigarettes. In a large group of people with HIV followed over more than a decade, the opposite was true: increasing cannabis use was linked to more drinking days and higher odds of hazardous drinking, while cutting back on cannabis was linked to less alcohol and cigarette use.

2025

Alcohol use disorder, but not current alcohol use, is associated with falls among people with HIV in care

Dai M, et al. AIDS

Summary: Looking at nearly 5,700 people with HIV in care, researchers found that having an alcohol use disorder diagnosis — but not alcohol use itself — was linked to a higher risk of falling in the past year, suggesting something other than being drunk in the moment may explain the connection.

2024

Association of Alcohol Use with COVID-19 Infection and Hospitalization Among People Living with HIV in the United States, 2020

Xia C, et al. AIDS and Behavior

Summary: Using health records from over 5,000 people with HIV in care during 2020, this study found that rates of COVID-19 diagnosis and hospitalization were somewhat higher among people who drank more, though the differences were not statistically significant — suggesting alcohol use had, at most, a modest effect on COVID-19 risk in this group.

2024

Evaluating the Sick Quitting Hypothesis for Frailty Status and Reducing Alcohol Use Among People With HIV in a Longitudinal Clinical Cohort Study

Ruderman SA, et al. Journal of the Association of Nurses in AIDS Care

Summary: "Sick quitting" describes people cutting back on alcohol because their health is declining, which can make alcohol look protective against frailty in research when it isn't. This study followed over 5,600 people with HIV and found that those who became frail were more likely to later quit or cut back on drinking, supporting sick quitting as an explanation researchers need to account for.

2023

Alcohol use and the longitudinal HIV care continuum for people with HIV who enrolled in care between 2011 and 2019

Lesko CR, et al. Annals of Epidemiology

Summary: Following nearly 1,800 people with HIV for several years after they entered care, this study found that those who reported unhealthy or moderate alcohol use at the start spent more time out of HIV care than those who didn't drink, though alcohol use wasn't linked to differences in time spent virally suppressed.

2023

The temporal relationship of alcohol use and subsequent self-reported health status among people with HIV

Pytell JD, et al. The American Journal of Medicine Open

Summary: In a study of over 5,000 people with HIV, heavy alcohol use was linked to worse self-reported health at later visits among women, but not among men — a pattern not explained by depression symptoms, suggesting the health effects of drinking may differ by gender.
